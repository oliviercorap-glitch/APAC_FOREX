1. FX Regimes

| Currency | Regime | Key Features | |----------|--------|--------------| | CNY | PBOC-managed float | Daily central parity (midpoint) set at 9:15am SGT; trading band of ±2% vs USD around the fix. The PBOC uses the fix as a signalling tool, with occasional "counter-cyclical factors" to smooth volatility. De facto peg to a USD-dominated basket, with gradual managed appreciation/depreciation trends. | | JPY | Free float | No formal band. BOJ intervenes only episodically (typically on disorderly moves, often USDJPY beyond 150 or below 100). Intervention requires MoF approval; BOJ executes. | | KRW | Free float (managed) | BOK intervenes via smoothing operations, but no target level. KRW is one of the most volatile Asian currencies vs USD, with high beta to global risk sentiment and tech trade flows. | | THB | Free float (managed) | BOT intervenes to curb excessive volatility, especially on appreciation (which hurts tourism/export competitiveness). THB has a structural tendency to strengthen on tourism receipts and current account surpluses. | | PHP | Free float (managed) | BSP intervenes to smooth sharp moves, but allows gradual depreciation trend to support export competitiveness and remittance-driven inflows. | | AUD | Free float | RBA does not intervene (last intervention 2011). AUD is a pure commodity currency, fully market-determined. |

2. Central Bank Mandates & Policy Tools

| Central Bank | Mandate | Relevant Tools for FX | |--------------|---------|----------------------| | PBOC | Price stability, growth, financial stability | Daily fix, reserve requirement ratio (RRR) for FX deposits, counter-cyclical factor, window guidance on banks' FX positions, offshore CNH liquidity management (via swap lines). | | BOJ | Price stability (2% inflation target) | Yield curve control (YCC) on 10Y JGBs, negative policy rate (until recently), outright JGB purchases. FX intervention only via MoF, funded by FX reserves. | | BOK | Price stability, financial stability | Policy rate, FX smoothing operations, macroprudential measures (e.g., FX forward position limits for banks). | | BOT | Price stability, financial stability | Policy rate, FX intervention (sterilised), capital flow management measures (e.g., non-resident baht account rules). | | BSP | Price stability, financial stability | Policy rate, FX intervention, reserve requirement changes, occasional capital flow measures. | | RBA | Price stability, full employment | Cash rate, QE (used during COVID), forward guidance. No FX intervention. |

3. Structural Drivers vs USD/EUR

  • Rate differentials: The USD is the dominant driver. When the Fed is hiking/on hold with high rates, APAC currencies (ex-JPY) tend to weaken. EURUSD moves affect all APAC crosses, but the direct EUR-APAC channel is weaker than USD-APAC. For your EUR reporting, the USD leg matters more than the EUR leg in most cases.

  • Capital flows: Portfolio flows (equity/bond) dominate short-term moves in KRW, THB, PHP. China's capital controls insulate CNY from hot money, but trade settlement flows and FDI matter. AUD is driven by global risk appetite and commodity prices, not local flows.

  • Commodity exposure (AUD): AUD is a proxy for iron ore, coal, LNG, and gold. When China's property/infrastructure cycle is strong, AUD strengthens. When global recession risk rises, AUD falls sharply. AUD is the highest-beta currency in your basket.

  • Export competitiveness dynamics:

  • CNY: PBOC historically resists sharp appreciation to protect manufacturing margins. A weaker CNY is a structural tailwind for Chinese exporters but a headwind for your group's CNY-invoiced costs.
  • JPY: Japan's structural current account surplus (from overseas investment income) supports JPY long-term, but the trade balance has turned negative on energy imports. JPY tends to weaken when global yields rise (carry trade).
  • KRW: High correlation with global tech cycle and semiconductor exports. KRW weakens when global tech demand falls.
  • THB: Tourism recovery and current account surplus are structural supports; BOT resists excessive strength.
  • PHP: Remittances (10% of GDP) and BPO exports provide a structural floor; BSP allows gradual depreciation to maintain competitiveness.

4. Practical Implications for Your Group

Margin Exposure

  • CNY invoicing: Your EUR costs vs CNY revenues create direct margin compression when CNY weakens vs EUR. The PBOC's managed float means moves are gradual but persistent—do not expect sharp reversals.
  • JPY/KRW/THB/PHP: These are more volatile. A 5–10% move in a quarter is possible. Your invoicing in these currencies while reporting in EUR means translation risk on receivables and transaction risk on cash flows.
  • AUD: High volatility, but also high correlation with global growth—which often aligns with your demand cycle. Natural hedge potential if you also source inputs from Australia.

Hedging Instruments

  • Forwards (NDFs for CNY, deliverable for others): Standard tool. For CNY, use onshore deliverable forwards (CNY) or offshore NDFs (CNH) depending on your entity structure. Tenors up to 12 months are liquid.
  • Currency swaps: Useful for longer-dated exposures (2–5 years) on CNY, KRW, THB. Allows you to swap fixed/floating cash flows without rolling forwards.
  • Natural hedging: Match invoicing currency with cost currency where possible. For example, if you invoice in AUD, consider sourcing raw materials from Australia. For THB, consider localising some production to offset baht revenues with baht costs.
  • Options: For high-volatility currencies (KRW, AUD), buying USD puts/APAC calls can cap downside while retaining upside. Premiums are significant; use only for tail-risk protection.

Timing Considerations

  • CNY: The daily fix at 9:15am SGT is a key event. Avoid executing large hedges within 30 minutes of the fix—volatility spikes.
  • JPY: Watch US Treasury yields and BOJ policy meetings. Intervention risk is highest when USDJPY moves >1% in a day.
  • KRW/THB/PHP: Monitor monthly trade data (export/import) and central bank meetings. These currencies tend to trend for 2–3 months in one direction before mean-reverting.
  • AUD: Key drivers are China PMI, iron ore prices, and RBA meetings. AUD often overshoots on both sides—use limit orders rather than market orders.
