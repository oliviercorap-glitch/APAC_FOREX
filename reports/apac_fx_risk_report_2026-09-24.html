Structural brief (background context, updated weekly)

APAC FX Structural Background Note

For: APAC CFO, Western Manufacturing Group From: Macro FX Strategy – Asia-Pacific Re: Structural reading grid for CNY, JPY, KRW, THB, PHP, AUD exposure Scope: Background only. No recent news, no current rate levels. To be read alongside live FX data and central bank updates.

1. FX Regimes

CNY – Managed float. The PBOC sets a daily central parity (fixing) against a basket with a strong USD weight, and the onshore spot (USD/CNY) trades within a band around that fix (historically ±2%). The offshore CNH trades freely and often leads the onshore rate. The regime is best described as a managed float with a strong policy preference for stability, especially against a trade-weighted basket. Expect the fix to lean against sharp moves, and expect administrative tools (e.g., counter-cyclical factors, reserve requirements on FX forwards) to be used at the margin.

JPY – Free float, intervention-capable. The yen is a fully free-floating, deeply liquid currency. The MOF decides on intervention (via the BOJ as agent), typically only in episodes of disorderly, speculative moves. The yen is structurally a funding currency and a safe-haven proxy, so it can appreciate sharply in risk-off episodes regardless of Japanese fundamentals.

KRW – Free float, heavy but informal smoothing. The won floats, but the BOK and authorities smooth disorderly moves and monitor offshore NDF positioning. Korea is highly exposed to global tech-cycle and risk sentiment, so KRW is a high-beta, pro-cyclical currency.

THB – Free float, managed against basket noise. The BOT allows market determination but has historically leaned against excessive volatility and has at times used measures to temper short-term capital inflows. Thailand runs persistent current-account surpluses, which structurally supports the baht.

PHP – Free float, light smoothing. The BSP lets the peso float and intervenes mainly to smooth volatility rather than target a level. The peso is driven by remittances, BPO receipts, import demand, and external financing conditions.

AUD – Free float, commodity-linked. The RBA does not target the exchange rate; AUD is one of the cleanest proxies for global risk appetite and commodity demand (iron ore, coal, base metals), with a strong correlation to China’s growth cycle.

2. Central Bank Mandates and Currency-Relevant Tools

  • PBOC: Multi-objective (growth, employment, price stability, financial stability, exchange-rate stability). Currency tools: daily fix, trading band, FX reserve intervention, macroprudential FX measures, CNH liquidity management.
  • BOJ: Price stability (2% target) with yield-curve control historically used to cap JGB yields. Currency is not a mandate, but ultra-loose policy has been the key structural JPY driver. Intervention is an MOF decision.
  • BOK: Inflation targeting plus financial stability. Uses policy rate and verbal/actual smoothing; monitors NDF and offshore positioning.
  • BOT: Inflation targeting with explicit concern for baht volatility. Uses policy rate, verbal guidance, and occasionally inflow-management measures.
  • BSP: Price stability and financial stability. Uses policy rate and light FX smoothing; remittance flows are a structural buffer.
  • RBA: Inflation targeting (2–3%) and full employment. Does not target AUD; rate differentials and terms of trade do the work.

3. Structural Drivers vs USD/EUR

  • Rate differentials: The dominant medium-term driver. USD-funded carry and Fed policy set the tone; EUR-USD dynamics then transmit into APAC crosses.
  • Capital flows: Portfolio flows into/out of EM Asia (KRW, THB, PHP) are highly sensitive to US yields and risk sentiment. CNY is additionally shaped by trade flows, FDI, and managed capital account tools.
  • Commodity exposure: AUD is the clearest commodity proxy; THB and PHP have softer commodity/terms-of-trade links; JPY and CNY are more policy- and trade-driven.
  • Export competitiveness: Asian authorities are structurally sensitive to rapid appreciation eroding export margins. This creates an asymmetric bias: tolerance for weakness is generally higher than for sharp strength.
  • EUR reporting angle: Because you report in EUR, your true exposure is the cross of each APAC currency against EUR, not just USD. USD is a useful secondary reference but not your accounting base.

4. Practical Implications for a Manufacturing Exporter

Margin exposure. Invoicing in CNY, JPY, KRW, THB, PHP, and AUD while reporting in EUR creates a layered mismatch: revenue in APAC currencies, cost base likely in EUR/USD, and translation into EUR. A weaker APAC currency versus EUR compresses reported margins even if local pricing is stable.

Hedging toolkit. - Forwards: The default for known receivables; liquid for CNY (onshore and offshore), JPY, KRW (NDF for non-residents), THB, PHP, AUD. - FX swaps: Useful for rolling hedges and managing liquidity across entities. - Natural hedging: Match APAC revenue with APAC costs (local sourcing, local debt, local capex) to reduce gross exposure before financial hedges. - Options: Consider collars or participating forwards where you want downside protection but are willing to cap upside, especially in high-carry pairs.

Timing considerations. - Hedge on a rolling layered basis rather than trying to time the market; policy-driven currencies (CNY) can gap on fix changes. - Be aware of intervention risk in JPY and smoothing risk in KRW, THB, PHP — these can distort short-term levels. - For AUD, align hedge ratios with the commodity/China cycle view. - Review hedge accounting treatment (IFRS 9) for EUR-reporting consolidation.

Bottom line: Treat CNY as policy-managed, JPY/KRW/THB/PHP as free floats with varying smoothing, and AUD as a commodity/risk proxy. Build a layered hedge program anchored to EUR, use USD as a reference only, and prioritize natural hedging where local cost structures allow.
