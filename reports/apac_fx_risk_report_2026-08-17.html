APAC FX Structural Background Note

For: APAC CFO, Western Manufacturing Group
Reporting currency: EUR | Secondary reference: USD
Invoicing currencies: CNY, JPY, KRW, THB, PHP, AUD

1. FX Regimes

| Currency | Regime | Key Features | |----------|--------|--------------| | CNY | PBOC-managed float (crawling peg to a basket) | Daily fixing (central parity) vs. USD; trading band of ±2% around fixing; PBOC steers via fixing and state-bank intervention | | JPY | Free float | No formal band; MOF/BOJ intervene sporadically (typically on disorderly moves); intervention via direct market sales/purchases | | KRW | Free float with managed intervention | BOK smooths volatility; authorities have a "three-stage" approach (verbal, macro-prudential, direct) | | THB | Free float with intervention | BOT intervenes to curb excessive volatility; capital-flow management measures used episodically | | PHP | Free float with intervention | BSP intervenes to smooth sharp moves; remittance flows are a structural anchor | | AUD | Pure free float | RBA does not target the level; intervention extremely rare (last used in crisis periods) |

2. Central Bank Mandates & Policy Tools

PBOC (China) – Mandate: price stability, growth, financial stability. Tools: daily fixing, reserve requirement ratios, open-market operations, window guidance, state-bank FX positioning. The PBOC prioritises stability of the exchange rate as a policy objective in itself, not just inflation.

BOJ (Japan) – Mandate: price stability (2% inflation target). Tools: yield curve control (YCC) on JGBs, negative policy rate (historically), QQE. FX intervention is a Ministry of Finance decision, executed by BOJ. The BOJ's ultra-loose policy historically drives JPY weakness via rate differentials.

BOK (Korea) – Mandate: price stability, financial stability. Tools: policy rate, FX market intervention (via swap lines, smoothing operations), macro-prudential measures (e.g., limits on FX derivatives for banks). BOK is sensitive to KRW volatility given high trade openness.

BOT (Thailand) – Mandate: monetary stability, financial system stability. Tools: policy rate, FX intervention, capital-flow measures (e.g., non-resident account rules). BOT has a de facto preference for a strong THB to curb imported inflation, but resists excessive appreciation that hurts exports.

BSP (Philippines) – Mandate: price stability, financial stability. Tools: policy rate, reserve requirements, FX intervention. BSP is heavily influenced by remittance inflows (structural USD supply) and manages PHP to avoid excessive appreciation that would hurt overseas worker remittances' local value.

RBA (Australia) – Mandate: price stability, full employment, economic welfare. Tools: policy rate, QE (used in crisis), no FX target. RBA's FX policy is non-interventionist; AUD is a pure commodity currency.

3. Structural Drivers vs. USD/EUR

Rate Differentials – The single largest driver. Historically: USD rates > APAC rates (except AUD in high-cycle periods). EUR rates have been lower than USD but higher than JPY/CNY. For your invoicing mix: - JPY is most sensitive to US-Japan rate spreads; when US yields rise, JPY weakens. - CNY is partially insulated by capital controls; its rate differential with USD matters but is secondary to PBOC policy. - AUD is a high-beta play on global rates; when risk appetite is strong, AUD strengthens.

Capital Flows – Portfolio flows dominate KRW, THB, PHP. Equity and bond inflows strengthen these currencies; outflows (risk-off) weaken them sharply. CNY is driven more by trade flows and FDI, less by hot money (due to controls). JPY is a funding currency – in risk-off, JPY often strengthens (carry unwind), opposite to other APAC currencies.

Commodity Exposure (AUD) – AUD is a proxy for iron ore, coal, LNG, and base metals. When China's property/infrastructure cycle is strong, AUD rallies. When global growth fears hit, AUD falls disproportionately.

Export Competitiveness Dynamics – All APAC economies (except Australia) are export-led. Their central banks tolerate gradual depreciation to maintain competitiveness, but resist sharp moves that trigger imported inflation or capital flight. KRW and THB are most sensitive to Chinese export competition; PHP is less so (services exports, remittances).

EUR Cross-Rates – EUR/APAC is a derived rate (EUR/USD × USD/APAC). Your EUR reporting means you face double volatility: EUR/USD moves plus USD/APAC moves. When EUR strengthens vs. USD, your APAC revenues in EUR terms improve, all else equal.

4. Practical Implications for a Manufacturing Exporter

Margin Exposure – You invoice in six currencies but report in EUR. A 10% depreciation of CNY vs. EUR directly cuts your EUR revenue by 10% on CNY-invoiced sales, unless offset by cost reductions. JPY and AUD are the most volatile; PHP and THB are less volatile but still meaningful. KRW is highly correlated with global tech cycles – your electronics supply chain exposure matters.

Hedging Instruments – Standard toolkit: - Forwards – most common; lock in rates for 3–12 months. Cost is the forward points (rate differential). For CNY, NDFs (non-deliverable forwards) may be needed if you cannot access onshore forwards. - Currency Swaps – useful for longer-dated exposures (2–5 years) or when you need to swap cash flows (e.g., convert AUD revenue into EUR debt service). - Natural Hedging – match invoicing currency with cost currency. If you source raw materials in CNY and sell in CNY, you reduce mismatch. Consider invoicing in EUR or USD where possible to shift FX risk to customers.

Timing Considerations – Do not hedge 100% of exposure at once. Layer hedges (e.g., 50% of next 6 months, 25% of next 6–12 months). Avoid hedging during extreme market moves – forward prices embed the current rate; if you hedge at a cyclical extreme, you lock in an unfavourable rate. For CNY, pay attention to PBOC fixing dates (daily) and major policy shifts (e.g., band changes). For JPY, monitor MOF intervention risk – intervention typically happens after a rapid move, not at a specific level.

Key Structural Warning – The CNY regime is the anchor for KRW, THB, and PHP. When CNY depreciates, these currencies tend to follow (competitiveness channel). When CNY is stable, they trade on their own fundamentals. Your hedging strategy should treat CNY as a regional bellwether, not just a single currency.
