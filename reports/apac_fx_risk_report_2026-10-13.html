APAC FX Structural Background Note

For: APAC CFO, Western Manufacturing Group Re: Structural FX framework for CNY, JPY, KRW, THB, PHP, AUD exposure Purpose: Background reading grid (no recent news or live rates)

1. FX Regimes

CNY (Chinese yuan / renminbi): A managed float. The PBOC sets a daily central parity (the "fix") against USD each morning, to which the onshore spot rate (USD/CNY) is allowed to trade within a band (historically ±2%). The fix is influenced by a basket of currencies and by policy discretion, so CNY is not a pure market price. A separate offshore market (CNH) trades more freely and often diverges from onshore CNY, which matters for hedging and settlement.

JPY (Japanese yen): Free-floating, with occasional Ministry of Finance (MoF) intervention executed by the BOJ, typically verbal or actual when moves are judged "excessive" or disorderly. JPY is a classic funding and safe-haven currency.

KRW (Korean won): Free-floating, but the BOK and authorities intervene to smooth volatility ("smoothing operations") rather than target a level. KRW is sensitive to global risk appetite and semiconductor/export cycles.

THB (Thai baht): Free-floating; the BOT intervenes to curb excessive volatility and has at times expressed concern about baht strength hurting exports and tourism. Thailand runs current-account surpluses and holds large reserves.

PHP (Philippine peso): Free-floating; the BSP intervenes mainly to smooth disorderly moves. PHP is supported by remittances and services exports (BPO), and is sensitive to import-driven trade deficits and oil prices.

AUD (Australian dollar): Free-floating; the RBA does not target a level and intervenes only in exceptional circumstances. AUD is a commodity/risk-proxy currency, closely linked to China demand, iron ore, and global growth.

2. Central Bank Mandates and Currency-Relevant Tools

  • PBOC: Multi-objective (growth, employment, price and financial stability). Currency tools: daily fix, trading band, reserve requirements on FX forwards, counter-cyclical factor, and direct/indirect intervention via state banks. Capital-flow management (quotas, macro-prudential rules) is a key lever.
  • BOJ: Price stability (2% target) with yield curve control historically. FX is formally an MoF responsibility; BOJ executes. Tools: rate policy, YCC/asset purchases, and FX intervention on MoF instruction.
  • BOK: Price stability and financial stability. Tools: policy rate, FX smoothing operations, macro-prudential FX rules (e.g., forward-position limits), and reserve management.
  • BOT: Price stability and financial stability. Tools: policy rate, FX intervention to reduce volatility, and measures on short-term capital flows.
  • BSP: Price stability and financial stability. Tools: policy rate, FX intervention, and reserve buffers. Remittance flows are a structural support.
  • RBA: Price stability and full employment; does not target the exchange rate. Tools: cash rate, and only rare FX intervention. AUD is left to market forces.

3. Structural Drivers vs USD/EUR

  • Rate differentials: Carry is a dominant driver. Higher US or Euro-area rates versus low-yielding JPY (and historically CHF) drive funding flows; higher APAC policy rates can attract portfolio inflows and support local currencies.
  • Capital flows: Portfolio and FDI flows, plus global risk sentiment, drive KRW, THB, PHP and AUD. Risk-off episodes typically strengthen USD and JPY, weaken AUD, KRW, THB, PHP.
  • Commodity exposure: AUD is the clearest commodity currency (iron ore, coal, metals, energy), linked to China's cycle. THB and PHP are more oil-import sensitive; KRW is tied to electronics/semis and oil imports.
  • Export competitiveness dynamics: Managed CNY and intervention-prone KRW/THB/PHP create asymmetry: authorities may resist rapid appreciation to protect exporters. This can cap upside for these currencies versus USD/EUR.
  • EUR cross: Because you report in EUR, USD/EUR moves matter as much as USD/Asia. A weaker USD versus EUR can compress EUR-translated Asian revenue even if local currencies are stable versus USD.

4. Practical Implications for a Manufacturing Exporter

Margin exposure: Invoicing in CNY, JPY, KRW, THB, PHP and AUD while reporting in EUR creates a two-layer mismatch: (i) local currency versus USD, and (ii) USD versus EUR. Even a "stable" managed CNY can move via the fix, and CNH/CNY divergence adds basis risk. JPY and AUD are the most volatile of the set; THB, PHP and KRW carry intervention risk that can create sudden, policy-driven moves.

Hedging instruments: - Forward contracts: Standard for CNY (onshore and offshore), JPY, KRW, THB, PHP, AUD. Note KRW and PHP onshore deliverability and documentation constraints; NDFs are common for KRW, and sometimes for CNY/THB/PHP where onshore access is limited. - FX swaps: Useful for short-term funding and rolling hedges; deep markets in JPY, AUD, CNY. - Natural hedging: Match local-currency costs (payroll, local suppliers, local debt) against local-currency revenue to reduce gross exposure. Consider local-currency borrowing where feasible. - Options: For uncertain volumes or to protect against tail moves (e.g., AUD, JPY) while retaining upside.

Timing considerations: - Hedge on a rolling layered basis rather than trying to time the fix or intervention windows. - Be aware of PBOC fix timing (daily), month-end/quarter-end flows, and Korean/Thai intervention patterns around volatility spikes. - For AUD, align hedge ratios with commodity/China cycle views; for JPY, watch rate differentials and safe-haven flows. - Because you report in EUR, consider whether to hedge USD/Asia directly or to manage EUR/USD separately, depending on your treasury's base and accounting treatment.

Governance: Define hedge ratios by currency, tenor and instrument; document policy for NDFs and CNH; monitor counterparty and country risk; and stress-test margins for 5–10% adverse moves in the most volatile pairs (JPY, AUD, KRW).
