Structural Background Note: Asia-Pacific Currency Regimes & Implications for a Western Manufacturing Exporter

1. FX Regime Overview

| Currency | Regime Type | Key Features | |----------|-------------|--------------| | CNY | Managed float (PBOC) | Daily fixing via “central parity”; ±2% trading band vs. USD; band widened from ±1% in 2014. PBOC sets reference rate based on prior close, basket weights (CFETS), and counter-cyclical factors. | | JPY | Free float (BOJ) | Fully convertible; BOJ intervenes only during disorderly moves (e.g., 2022–2023 USD/JPY spikes). No explicit band. | | KRW | Free float (BOK) | Market-determined but BOK actively smoothes volatility via spot intervention and forward swaps. Korea maintains a “free-floating” label under IMF Article IV. | | THB | Managed float (BOT) | BOT intervenes to limit excessive volatility and build reserves; no explicit band. Capital account partially liberalised. | | PHP | Managed float (BSP) | BSP intervenes to smooth sharp moves; reserves used as buffer. PHP is among the more volatile Asian currencies. | | AUD | Free float (RBA) | No intervention since 1983; fully market-driven. Highly correlated with commodity prices and risk appetite. |

2. Central Bank Mandates & Policy Tools

  • PBOC (China): Dual mandate – price stability and growth. Tools: daily fixing, reserve requirement ratios (RRR), medium-term lending facility (MLF) rates, window guidance, and direct spot intervention via state banks. Currency stability is a policy priority to support trade and capital flow management.
  • BOJ (Japan): Price stability (2% inflation target) and financial system stability. Tools: yield curve control (YCC) on 10-year JGBs (recently relaxed), negative policy rate (ended March 2024), and occasional FX intervention via MOF directive. BOJ’s ultra-loose stance historically weakens JPY.
  • BOK (Korea): Price stability (2% target) and financial stability. Tools: base rate, FX swaps, and macroprudential measures (e.g., limits on foreign bond holdings). BOK intervenes to prevent excessive KRW weakness that fuels import inflation.
  • BOT (Thailand): Inflation targeting (1–3% range) with flexibility for growth. Tools: policy rate, FX intervention, and capital flow management measures (e.g., bond withholding tax). BOT tolerates gradual THB depreciation to support exports.
  • BSP (Philippines): Price stability (2–4% target) and financial stability. Tools: policy rate, reserve requirements, and direct FX intervention. BSP has limited firepower relative to GDP; PHP is sensitive to remittance flows and oil prices.
  • RBA (Australia): Price stability (2–3% inflation target) and full employment. Tools: cash rate, quantitative easing (used 2020–2021), and forward guidance. RBA does not target AUD level; AUD is a pure market price.

3. Structural Drivers vs. USD/EUR

  • Rate Differentials: USD and EUR rates have risen sharply since 2022, compressing carry in Asia. JPY remains the lowest-yielding G10 currency; AUD offers moderate carry. THB and PHP have seen rate hikes but still lag USD. KRW and CNY rates are set domestically with limited correlation to Fed/ECB.
  • Capital Flows: Asia ex-Japan has experienced portfolio outflows during risk-off episodes (e.g., 2022 Ukraine shock). China’s capital controls limit CNY convertibility; offshore CNH is more volatile. Japan’s large current account surplus provides structural JPY support, but yield-seeking outflows (carry trade) weaken it.
  • Commodity Exposure (AUD): AUD is a proxy for iron ore, coal, and LNG prices. A China slowdown directly pressures AUD via lower commodity demand. EUR/AUD is also sensitive to risk appetite (AUD weakens during global stress).
  • Export Competitiveness Dynamics: China, Korea, Thailand, and Philippines compete in manufacturing exports. A weaker CNY often triggers competitive depreciation pressure on KRW, THB, and PHP (the “Asian currency bloc” effect). Japan’s export sector benefits from a weak JPY, but BOJ’s eventual normalisation could reverse this.

4. Practical Implications for a Manufacturing Exporter

Margin Exposure

  • CNY, JPY, KRW, THB, PHP are typically invoiced in local currency for domestic sales, but your group invoices exports in these currencies. Revenue is in local currency, while costs (labour, materials) are partly in EUR or USD. A depreciation of the invoicing currency against EUR erodes EUR-reported margins.
  • AUD invoicing adds commodity cycle risk: AUD tends to weaken when global demand falls, compounding revenue pressure.
  • USD as secondary reference means your group may also face translation risk on USD-denominated debt or intercompany loans.

Typical Hedging Instruments

  • Forward contracts: Most liquid for JPY, AUD, KRW; less liquid for THB and PHP (longer tenors may be costly). Use to lock in EUR/local currency rates for 3–6 month receivables.
  • Currency swaps: Useful for hedging longer-term exposures (e.g., CNY or KRW loans). Cross-currency basis swaps for THB and PHP are thin; use NDFs instead.
  • Natural hedging: Match local-currency revenues with local-currency costs (e.g., sourcing materials in THB if invoicing in THB). Consider setting up local treasury centres in Singapore or Hong Kong for netting.

Timing Considerations

  • CNY: PBOC fixing changes daily at 9:15 am Beijing time. Avoid hedging during fixing windows (volatility spikes). Use NDFs for offshore hedging.
  • JPY: BOJ intervention typically occurs after USD/JPY moves >5% in a short period. Hedge JPY exposures when volatility is low (e.g., after BOJ policy meetings).
  • KRW, THB, PHP: Central banks often intervene to slow depreciation, not reverse it. Hedge when spot is near perceived intervention levels (e.g., BOK’s “smoothing” zone).
  • AUD: RBA meetings and China data releases (PMI, trade) are key triggers. Hedge AUD/EUR after RBA rate decisions when liquidity is highest.
