Asia-Pacific FX Structural Background Note

For: APAC CFO, Western Manufacturing Group
Reporting currency: EUR | Secondary reference: USD
Invoicing currencies: CNY, JPY, KRW, THB, PHP, AUD

1. FX Regimes by Currency

| Currency | Regime | Key Feature | |----------|--------|-------------| | CNY | PBOC-managed float | Daily central parity (midpoint) vs. USD; trading band ±2% around parity. The PBOC sets the fix each morning, guiding the band via state-bank intervention and window guidance. | | JPY | Free float | No formal band; BOJ intervenes only sporadically (typically via MoF orders) to counter disorderly moves. | | KRW | Free float (managed) | BOK intervenes to smooth volatility, but no explicit target. Capital-flow measures used episodically. | | THB | Free float (managed) | BOT intervenes to curb excessive speculation; occasional capital-control measures. | | PHP | Free float (managed) | BSP intervenes to smooth volatility, not to defend a level. | | AUD | Pure free float | RBA does not target the level; intervention extremely rare (last major episodes in 2008 and 2011). |

Key nuance: CNY is the only currency with a de jure band; the rest are de facto managed floats with varying intervention intensity. THB and PHP tend to see more frequent central bank presence than JPY or AUD.

2. Central Bank Mandates & Policy Tools

  • PBOC (China): Dual mandate of growth stability and FX stability. Tools: daily fixing, reserve requirement ratio (RRR) for FX deposits, counter-cyclical factor in the fix, state-bank FX swaps, and moral suasion. The PBOC prioritises orderly depreciation/appreciation over free market clearing.
  • BOJ (Japan): Price stability (2% inflation target) and financial stability. Tools: yield curve control (YCC) on JGBs (now largely relaxed), negative policy rate (historically), and FX intervention via MoF. BOJ does not target the yen; intervention is rare and only for excessive volatility.
  • BOK (Korea): Price stability and financial stability. Tools: policy rate, FX market intervention (via swaps and outright sales/purchases), and macroprudential measures (e.g., limits on FX derivatives positions for banks).
  • BOT (Thailand): Inflation targeting (1–3% range) and financial stability. Tools: policy rate, FX intervention (sterilised), and capital-flow management measures (e.g., non-resident baht account rules).
  • BSP (Philippines): Inflation targeting (2–4%) and external stability. Tools: policy rate, FX intervention, and reserve accumulation. BSP is more tolerant of gradual depreciation to support export competitiveness.
  • RBA (Australia): Inflation targeting (2–3%) and full employment. Tools: policy rate, quantitative easing (used in crisis), and FX intervention only in exceptional circumstances. RBA explicitly does not target the AUD level.

3. Structural Drivers vs. USD/EUR

  • Rate differentials: The single largest driver. When US/EU rates are high relative to APAC, capital flows out of APAC into USD/EUR assets, pressuring APAC currencies. JPY is most sensitive to US-Japan yield spreads; AUD to US-Australia spreads; CNY to US-China spreads (though capital controls dampen the pass-through).
  • Capital flows: Portfolio flows dominate short-term moves in KRW, THB, and PHP (high foreign ownership of local bonds/equities). CNY is driven more by trade flows and FDI, with portfolio flows restricted. AUD is driven by global risk appetite and commodity prices, not just rates.
  • Commodity exposure (AUD): AUD is a proxy for global growth and iron ore/coal/LNG prices. A China slowdown or global recession hits AUD disproportionately. EUR/AUD cross is also sensitive to European energy import costs.
  • Export competitiveness dynamics: KRW and THB are highly sensitive to global electronics and auto supply chains; PHP to services exports (BPO) and remittances; CNY to global manufacturing demand. A persistently strong USD tends to be resisted by PBOC (via fixing) and BOK/BOT (via intervention) to protect export margins.
  • EUR-specific: EUR/Asia crosses are less liquid than USD/Asia. EUR weakness (e.g., from energy shocks) tends to strengthen APAC currencies vs. EUR even if they are stable vs. USD, creating invoicing mismatches.

4. Practical Implications for a Manufacturing Exporter

Margin Exposure

  • CNY invoicing: If you invoice in CNY and report in EUR, your margin is exposed to CNY/EUR. CNY is managed vs. USD, so CNY/EUR moves are largely a function of EUR/USD. A weak EUR (strong USD) compresses your EUR margin even if CNY is stable vs. USD.
  • JPY/KRW/THB/PHP invoicing: These are more volatile vs. EUR. JPY can move 10–15% in a quarter on rate differentials. KRW and THB are prone to sudden capital-flow reversals. PHP is relatively stable but can depreciate gradually.
  • AUD invoicing: AUD/EUR is highly correlated with commodity prices and global risk. If you invoice in AUD and your costs are in EUR, a commodity price slump directly hits margins.

Typical Hedging Instruments

  • Forward contracts: Most common. Lock in a rate for up to 12 months (24 for CNY, AUD, JPY). Cost is the forward points (interest rate differential). For CNY, use offshore (CNH) forwards if onshore access is limited.
  • Currency swaps: Useful for longer-dated exposures (2–5 years) or if you have matching cash flows (e.g., pay CNY, receive EUR). Cross-currency swaps allow you to swap fixed/floating interest rates and principal.
  • Natural hedging: Match invoicing currency with cost currency where possible. If you have CNY costs and CNY revenues, you are naturally hedged. Consider shifting some production or procurement to the same currency zone as your major sales.
  • Options: Useful for uncertain cash flows (e.g., tender-based sales). Buy EUR puts/APAC calls to protect against APAC appreciation while retaining upside if APAC depreciates.

Timing Considerations

  • Fix your hedge horizon to your order book: If you have 6-month firm orders, hedge 6 months out. Do not hedge speculative future sales.
  • Avoid hedging CNY via USD only: If you report in EUR, hedge CNY/EUR directly (via CNH/EUR forwards) rather than CNY/USD + USD/EUR, to reduce basis risk.
  • Be aware of fix timing: PBOC fixes at 9:15am China time; large moves in USD/CNY overnight are often partially absorbed in the fix. Trade after the fix to avoid adverse selection.
  • Quarter-end and year-end effects: APAC central banks (especially BOT, BSP) tend to smooth volatility around major data releases and local holidays. Liquidity thins during Golden Week (China), Lunar New Year, and Japanese holidays—avoid large hedges during these windows.
